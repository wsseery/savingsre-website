Prequalification vs Preapproval Letter: What Each Means and Why Sellers Ask

The prequalification vs preapproval letter question comes up the moment you start shopping. Here is what the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says the difference is, and what to ask your lender.

If you are getting ready to buy, you will hear two terms, often used as if they were the same thing. On a prequalification vs preapproval letter comparison, the labels matter less than what sits behind them. This note walks through what the CFPB says, why a listing agent keeps asking for “the letter,” and the one question that tells you the most.

Two words, used differently by different lenders

According to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, lenders use the two words differently. Some lenders issue a prequalification letter based on information you report that they have not verified, and a preapproval letter only after they verify it.

Because the labels are not uniform, you cannot assume that a letter called “preapproval” at one lender means the same as at another. What counts is whether the lender checked what you told them.

What either letter does and does not say

Either way, the letter says the lender is generally willing to lend up to a certain amount, based on certain assumptions. It is not a guaranteed loan offer. Treat it as a starting point for your search, not a commitment that the loan will close.

Why the seller’s agent asks for it

In the CFPB’s words, the letter “gives the seller confidence that you will be able to get financing.” A seller weighing an offer wants some sign that the buyer can actually get a loan. When two offers look alike, a letter backed by verified information can carry more weight.

The question worth asking your lender

Ask this: is this letter based on verified information, or only on what I told you? The answer tells you more than the label does. So skip the label and ask what was checked. A letter based on verified information tells a seller more.

One more point from the CFPB: lenders may check your credit for either one.

The SavingsRE take
  • The two terms are used differently by different lenders, so do not rely on the label alone.
  • Either letter is a general willingness to lend up to a certain amount, based on certain assumptions. It is not a guaranteed loan offer.
  • Ask whether the letter rests on verified information or only on what you reported.
